## What the FSL permits at no charge

The examples below are illustrative, **not exhaustive**. Many real-world uses fall under "Permitted Purpose" in the LICENSE; the examples below are common patterns that almost always qualify.

You can use Fracta freely under the FSL in any of the following situations:

- **Internal use, any organization size.** Run Fracta on your own infrastructure to support your own work — security operations, knowledge management, internal automation, research, threat hunting, anything. This is true regardless of whether your organization is a charity, a startup, a Fortune 500 enterprise, or anything in between.
- **Modifying Fracta for your own use.** Patch bugs, customize the configuration, build internal extensions or strategies. Your modifications remain subject to the FSL.
- **Building strategies, plugins, and integrations on top of Fracta.** These are derivative works and are also covered by the FSL by default.
- **Using Fracta to do your own work for clients, internally.** For example, a consultancy may run Fracta on its own laptops or servers to investigate data and produce reports for clients, provided that the deliverable to the client is the analysis, report, or recommendations — not Fracta itself.
- **Helping a client deploy Fracta in their own environment, where the client uses Fracta directly and is themselves an FSL licensee** in compliance with these terms. This is the "professional services to a licensee" carve-out in the FSL.
- **Non-commercial education and research.**

<Note>
This list is illustrative, not exhaustive. Any use of Fracta that is not a "Competing Use" as defined in the LICENSE is permitted. If your situation isn't covered by the examples above, the LICENSE text still controls — the test is whether your use is a Competing Use, not whether it appears on this list.
</Note>

## What requires a commercial license

These examples are also **illustrative, not exhaustive**. The controlling test is the "Competing Use" definition in the LICENSE: a commercial product or service that substitutes for Fracta, substitutes for any commercial product or service the author offers using Fracta, or offers the same or substantially similar functionality as Fracta.

Common patterns that require a commercial license include:

- **Offering Fracta (or a fork, repackage, or derivative) as a hosted product or managed service to customers.** Whether you call it a SaaS, a managed-service offering, or a "platform" — if customers are paying to access Fracta-as-a-service, you need a commercial license.
- **Embedding Fracta into a commercial product or service such that Fracta is a substantial portion of what you're selling.** Rule of thumb: if removing Fracta from your offering would fundamentally change what you're selling, you probably need a commercial license.
- **Productizing Fracta as a branded consulting offering** — e.g. "[Your Firm]'s Swarm Threat Platform powered by Fracta."
- **Any other arrangement that constitutes a "Competing Use"** under the LICENSE, even if it is not specifically listed above. The list here is not a safe harbor for uses that fall outside it but still meet the Competing Use definition.

<Warning>
If your intended use does not appear on either list but it might involve making Fracta available to others in a commercial context, the safest approach is to apply the test in the LICENSE directly — or just ask.
</Warning>

## After 2 years

Per the FSL terms, each release of Fracta automatically converts to the Apache License 2.0 on the second anniversary of its publication. Releases that have crossed that two-year mark may be used under Apache-2.0 like any other permissively-licensed software, with no commercial license required.
